The second book

The war of the six kingdoms

What follows is the account of the taking back of the kingdoms, west to east, in the order they were taken. It was set down at Greenhaven from the mouths of men who were there, some years after, and where the leaf is burnt we have said so.

I Greenhaven

Held from the first day to the last. Where the pencil was found.

Greenhaven was never taken, and so there is little to tell of it as a battlefield and a good deal to tell of it as a place. It was ruled country in the old sense: the fields laid out in straight lines, north to south, and a man knew his own furrow from his neighbour's by the way the line wobbled. The people of it were paper. They were thin by the end of winter, they burned easily, and they were — by every account that has come down to us — patient.

The pencil was found in the third furrow of the east field by Iries, who was nineteen and not otherwise remarkable. What it did was learned within the hour. Whatever is drawn and then named comes alive, and the name is the whole of the wish: he drew a line, wrote wall under it, and it stood up, and it was a wall. Before that month was out Greenhaven had hedges that mended themselves overnight, a plough-horse with six legs, because more legs run faster, and a doctor drawn over three evenings, coat and hands and the lines of the face, who walked house to house through the fever of that spring, put down eleven cases and lost none of them. He was the first thing Iries made slowly, and the village noticed that it mattered.

They also learned, by accident, the law that decided the whole war. A boy carried a drawn goose east over the line into Dogear Downs and it stopped being a goose. It did not die; it lay in the road and was paper, and the ink on it had not moved at all. A drawing lives only on ground its maker holds. Everything that follows in this book is men working around that one sentence.

¶ Greenhaven never fell. It is the only leaf in this book with no roll of the dead at the foot of it.

Nota The furrow is still shown to visitors. It is the wrong furrow.

II Dogear Downs

The first taken. Nine days, and the first crossing of a border.

Dogear Downs is folded country. It was always somewhat folded — the downs run in long soft creases and the corners of the fields lift in wet weather — but the roaches worked it. They went along the border turning the corners down, one after another, a hand's breadth at a time, and where a corner has been turned down the ground beneath it is not ground any more. It is the back of the page.

That made the border a trap rather than a line. A column crossing at any speed put its foot on a flap; the flap lifted; the column went over onto the reverse, where nothing is drawn and nothing grows. Four hundred of the drawn went that way in the first week. They had been made in Greenhaven and they had left it, so they were paper before they landed, and they did not get up. Nor could Iries simply draw a bridge: a bridge drawn in Greenhaven is alive in Greenhaven and stops dead at the line.

What he did instead was to draw a wall on his own ground — flat on its back, the longest thing he had made, sixty yards of it — and then push it over the border. The far half went dead as it crossed, exactly as the goose had, and dead paper lies flat and takes a man's weight. They crossed Dogear on a wall that had stopped being a wall. The archers never crossed at all; they stood in Greenhaven and shot, because an arrow is not alive. An arrow is only an arrow, and it goes where it is sent. The keep fell on the ninth day, and the moment it fell the ground was his, and the pencil worked there, and after that it was quick.

¶ Nine days. Four hundred and eleven of the drawn. Of the paper-born, eleven, of whom four were carters.

III Creasemarch

Fought along a fold-line, north to south. Twenty-two days.

Creasemarch has a fold through the middle of it, north to south, old and deep, and the war there was fought along that fold and nowhere else. A crease does one particular thing to a drawn creature: it bends it. Anything with a closed loop in its shape — a shield, a ring of mail, a barrel, a good helmet — cracks when it is carried over a crease, since a closed loop is armour precisely because it will not give, and a fold asks it to give. Our heaviest companies could not cross. Our lightest could, and our lightest died.

The western half of the kingdom came to us in four days and after that we stopped. The roaches knew the crease better than we did and had built on both lips: towers on their side, towers on ours before we took them, each with a scribble beneath it that the scouts read as tower and nothing further. They did not need to hold the middle of that kingdom. The middle held itself.

So Iries drew a dragon. He spent a night and most of the following day on it, which was unlike him; the shape decides the strength, detail makes a masterpiece, and he put four hours into one wing alone. The camp record says he did not speak while he drew. The men called her the Long Wing; written under her, in the pencil's own hand, was dragon, and nothing else. She never crossed the crease once. She did not have to. She went up over our own lip and breathed across it, and fire, like an arrow, is not alive and does not care whose ground it falls on. The far towers burned from the lip inward for six days. On the twenty-second Iries put a bomb into the fold itself — spiked all over, because spikes are damage — and let the fold carry it south the way a fold carries anything laid in it. The trench that had been the front became the thing that broke the front.

¶ Twenty-two days. Two hundred and six of the drawn. Of the paper-born, thirty. The crease is still there; it is the road now.

Nota Of the Long Wing there is more in the fourth book, and most of it is wrong.

IV Smudgewood

Where nothing holds its edge. Sixteen days, and no roll that can be read.

In Smudgewood nothing holds its edge. A line drawn there is soft within the hour and gone by the second day, and since the shape decides the strength, a thing that loses its shape loses what it was for. The roaches did not fight us at all for the first eight days. They waited. Every masterpiece we had walked in from Creasemarch came apart by itself: the good armour blurred until the closed loops opened, the spears went round at the point, and the Long Wing was grounded, because a wing gone soft will not hold air.

Iries changed what the army drew. Nothing fine. Blunt, thick, stupid shapes, drawn fast and drawn again. The pencil never left his hand — there was only ever the one, and one man to hold it — but a shape already named can be kept in shape by any hand with charcoal, and the redrawing companies walked the line all day going back over the outlines of their own men. A man who had been gone over twice by noon counted himself lucky. And Iries had found the thing that saved the campaign, which is that a smudge keeps its name. An archer with no face is still an archer. A wall that has gone to fog is still, in law and in fact, a wall. So he stopped drawing one good wall and drew nine hundred bad ones, one behind another, and let the wood eat them at its own pace.

The roaches answered with erasers. They drew them with a great many legs, because more legs is faster, and sent them at our lines in the dark; where an eraser had been there was nothing at all — no body, no mark, no name to put in the roll. Iries beat them with cake. He drew one the size of a barn on the last yard of wood we held, named it, and left the line in front of it open. Cake calls a roach the way nothing else in the world does. They came past their own erasers to get at it. The erasers stopped where they stood and did not move again; an eraser with nothing behind it does not know where to go.

¶ Sixteen days. The roll of the dead for Smudgewood cannot be read. It was written in Smudgewood.

Nota The redrawing companies took no oath and are in no roll. They are said to have been mostly women, and mostly from Dogear.

V Blotmire

Drowned in spilled ink. Forty days. The worst ground of the war.

Blotmire was not a mire. It was a kingdom with a river in it and two decent towns, and the roaches drew ink across it and wrote ink underneath, and the ink came alive; and living ink does not stay where it is poured. It took the river first, then the low fields, then Ferrel and then Coyle, at about the pace of a man walking. By the time our column came up out of Smudgewood the whole kingdom was one blot forty miles across, and it was still going west.

Out of it they drew a dragon. This is the only dragon in the war that the roaches made, and it was not a fine drawing: one stroke for each wing, no scales, no eye, nothing done twice. It did not need to be fine. It was enormous, and bigger is tougher, and a thing made of ink has no edge on it to break. Iries would not send the Long Wing over the mire and she did not ask to go; a drawing out over ground its maker does not hold is a drawing falling. She worked from the Smudgewood side instead, burning the ink in strips along the border, west to east, and the drake thinned as its own ground thinned. On the fourteenth day it drew her out. She went after it across the line, four hundred yards over open ink, and stopped in the air. The two of them came down together into what was left. The mire closed over. Neither came up again.

She had burned two hundred yards of the near shore dry, and that was where the crossing began. The rest of Blotmire was taken on the dead. Past the burnt shore nothing could be drawn, because we did not hold the mire; nothing drawn in Smudgewood stayed alive past the border. But dead paper floats. They laid their own stopped drawings down on the ink, rank after rank, walked over them, and lifted them again behind. Blotmire was crossed on things that had been alive that morning. The men who did it did not care to talk about it afterwards, and the scribes of Greenhaven have not asked them twice.

¶ Forty days. One thousand nine hundred of the drawn, and the Long Wing. Of the paper-born, ninety-four, and the whole of the town of Ferrel, which was not counted.

Nota Ink lifted from the mire still will not write. It only spreads.

VI The Roach Throne

The last. One morning, and the breaking of their pencil.

The Roach Throne is a room, not a country: a hall of chewed paper standing on a hill of chewed paper, where everything had been eaten once and spat out again into a shape. They had held it since the beginning, which meant they could draw inside it as freely as we drew in Greenhaven, and they had had years to do it. There were doors that were mouths. There were a hundred towers in a yard that would not properly have held ten. Iries could not put a single mark on any of it, because he held not one foot of that ground, and until he held a foot of it he had no army there at all.

So the last battle of the war was fought over a yard of bare dirt inside their own gate, and he won it with the oldest thing in this book. He drew a cake in Blotmire, on ground he held, close up against the line — a large one, and finely done, because this once the detail mattered. When they came out for it the gate stood open and the yard behind them was empty. Twelve men walked in and stood on it. That was enough; it was ground held. Everything he had drawn and left standing along the Blotmire line came alive as it came onto that held ground, all of it at once, and the rest took until noon.

Their pencil was lying on the arm of the throne. It was shorter than ours and it had been chewed at the end. He did not draw anything to break it — there is no word that undoes a pencil, and if there is one, nobody has found it since. He broke it on his knee, in front of the ones still standing, and the hall went quiet, and then a great deal of what was in that hall stopped being anything at all. That was the end of the war. It was the ninth month, and it had rained in the night.

¶ One morning. One cake. One pencil, broken by hand, there being no word for it.

Nota The two halves are kept apart, in two houses, by agreement of both.
