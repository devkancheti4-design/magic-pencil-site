How the pencil was found

Copied at Greenhaven out of the parish book, in three hands, a long while after.

Before the pencil there was only the flat country, and it was enough. The paper people of Greenhaven kept bees, mended their own edges when they tore, and wrote very little down, since little happened that was worth the ink. A man was known by his creases. Merrow Tay, the oldest woman in the parish, had been folded nine times and could not stand straight, and went to market on a board, and complained of the road. We had names for eleven kinds of weather and no name at all for war.

Iries mended fences. On a wet morning in the second week of the thaw he walked up to the head of the west field to re-hang a gate, and found the pencil lying in the cart-rut, half under water, with the water standing off it on every side and not touching it. It was a hand’s breadth long. It had been sharpened with a knife, badly, by somebody in a hurry, and there were teeth-marks near the middle. He put it behind his ear and hung the gate first. He has said since that this was the last ordinary hour of his life, and that he spent it on a gate.

Note added in the margin: ¶The Dogear copy reads six inches here. That is a roach measure and means nothing on this side of the fold. A hand’s breadth, and the hand was his.

For three days it was a pencil and nothing else. He drew a latch on the back of a tithe-list and the latch stayed where it was drawn. Then, out of the habit of a man who labels his tools, he wrote the word latch beneath it. The drawing stood up off the paper, and was iron, and was cold, and worked. The law is five words long and he had it by evening. The name is the wish. What the name leaves open, the drawing settles: a thing is as strong as it is big, as quick as it has legs, and a line closed all the way round will turn a blade.

Note added in the margin: This much is said at every muster, and the youngest present has to say it back.

The first thing he made on purpose was a dog, because the fox had been at the hens. He drew it badly — four legs, a wedge for a head — and wrote dog, and it came off the page with its ears already up and went straight under the gate after the fox. It was not a good drawing. It was a very good dog. It lived eleven years and is buried at the head of the west field, under the gate he was hanging the morning he found the pencil. That is the whole of the first year: a man, a dog, and a field nobody wanted.

There was a second pencil. We did not know that, and it is the reason for everything that follows. The account of the other side is not ours, and what is certain of it is little: that it lay in the wet under the eastern boarding, where dropped things go; that the roaches had it a whole season before they understood it, and used it a season more to prop a door. Then one of them scratched a shape, and wrote king under the shape, and the shape got up.

Note added in the margin: ¶Compare fol. xiv, the taking of Blotmire, for what a pencil does to a country when it is held badly.

They worked one night. They worked quickly and they were not careful, and by morning there were kingdoms east of us where there had been fields. Dogear Downs, with every corner turned down. Creasemarch, along the fold. Smudgewood, which will not hold an edge. Blotmire, under its own spilled ink. And over those four, the Roach Throne, drawn last and drawn largest. Six kingdoms on the paper by daylight, and one of them ours. None of it was well made. All of it was alive. The war is in those two sentences.

Note added in the margin: ¶Others say it was a Tuesday, and that it took three nights and not one. The parish kept no clock and had no reason to.

What we lost first was not ground. It was the writing. A drawing stands up only on ground its maker holds, and as the parishes went over the words already set down on their paper stood up for the roaches instead. So the parishes stopped keeping books, and then there were no books to lose when the parishes went. The mills went in the spring. Eleven hundred of us went between the thaw and the harvest, and most of their names are not in this volume, which is a second death and the one we might have prevented. We kept the bees. We kept Greenhaven, which was never taken. And we kept the first pencil, which is the only reason there is anything further to write.
